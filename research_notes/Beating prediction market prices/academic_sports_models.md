# Academic evidence on predictive models vs. sportsbook / exchange closing prices (tennis, MLB, related), 2015–2026

Scope note for the report writer: every result below is tagged as one of
**[vs-market]** (measured against bookmaker/exchange prices: ROI vs odds, CLV, or a proper score compared to market-implied probability),
**[outcome-only]** (accuracy/Brier vs results with no market comparison), or **[claim-only]** (abstract-level claim I could not verify numerically).
Where I only reached an abstract or a secondary summary, I say so. Papers whose full text I read: Sipko (2015, Imperial thesis), Hubáček & Šír (arXiv 2010.12508 / IJF 2023), Choe & Ramdas (arXiv 2110.00115 / Operations Research), the Tennis Abstract 2016 US Open comparison, the Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe Polymarket paper (abstract), and the arXiv systematic review (2410.21484, first ~200k chars).

## Key question 1 — Which published models beat Pinnacle/Betfair (or other) closing prices in tennis? Edge, sample, fee treatment

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed tennis paper I found demonstrates a statistically robust, fee-adjusted edge over Pinnacle or Betfair *closing* prices on a large out-of-sample set. Rating-based models (Elo variants, Bradley–Terry, WElo, point-level Markov) consistently come in *just below* the bookmaker on accuracy/Brier (bookmaker ~69–77% accuracy, Brier ~0.17–0.20 depending on sample; best models 0.5–3 points lower). The positive-ROI claims that exist (Sipko 4.35%, Knottenbelt 3.8%, Lisi 15.9%, Cornman 3.3%/match, Angelini et al. "meaningfully profitable") are single-window backtests, mostly against Pinnacle or Bet365 *pre-match* odds of unspecified timing (not explicitly closing), with no commission modelling, no significance test against data snooping, and in the one study that did control for data snooping (Lyócsa & Výrost 2018) the apparent profits disappeared.

### Cited Findings

**Benchmark studies: bookmaker beats or ties the best models**
- Kovalchik (2016, JQAS, "Searching for the GOAT of tennis win prediction") tested 11 published forecasting models on 2,395 ATP singles matches from the 2014 season using bookmaker predictions as the benchmark; FiveThirtyEight-style Elo was among the most accurate, ranking second behind market consensus odds at ~70% overall accuracy. Elo reached 75% on matches involving the highest-ranked players (competitive with bookmakers) and all models were 10–20 points less accurate for lower-ranked players. **[vs-market, accuracy/log-loss; no betting ROI reported in the summaries I could access]** — [JQAS DOI 10.1515/jqas-2015-0059](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jqas-2015-0059/html) (full text not reachable; figures from the abstract and the secondary summary in [arXiv 2609.07617](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.07617)).
- Tennis Abstract (Sackmann, 2016 US Open, 117 matches, Pinnacle odds with overround removed): Pinnacle accuracy 76.92%, Brier .172, calibration .015, resolution .093; FiveThirtyEight Elo 75.21% / Brier .171 / calib .003 / res .082; Tennis Abstract Elo 74.36% / .182; ATP ranking formula 72.65% / .185; Riles Elo 70.09% / .211. Pinnacle and 538 essentially tied on Brier; Pinnacle was underconfident in its 60–69% bin (91% correct). **[vs-market, Brier; tiny sample]** — [Tennis Abstract](https://www.tennisabstract.com/blog/?p=2129)
- Nottingham Trent working paper (2019) on the four Grand Slams: betting odds outperformed all Elo variants on accuracy and calibration for men; for women a weighted composite of overall and surface-specific Elo was best on accuracy. **[vs-market, accuracy/calibration]** — [NTU IRep 42038](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/42038)
- Graph-neural-network study (test period 2022–2025): Pinnacle implied probability ("PS") 68.7% accuracy, Brier 0.198, "notably superior overall"; weighted Elo 65.8%, Brier 0.214. **[vs-market, Brier]** — [arXiv 2510.20454](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.20454)
- Penn, Michael & Bhatt (2025, dynamic graph model for pre-tournament odds, 2024–2025 majors): bookmakers predicted 1,249/1,686 (74.1%) correctly, the model 1,237 (73.5%), rankings 1,173 (69.7%); no significant difference between model and bookmakers (p = 0.40). No betting simulation. **[vs-market, accuracy only]** — [arXiv 2508.15956](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.15956)
- Wilkens (2021, J. Sports Analytics, "Sports prediction and betting models in the machine learning age: the case of tennis"; ATP+WTA singles 2010–2019; LR, RF, SVM, ANN, GBM): accuracy ceiling ~70%; "most of the relevant information is embedded in the betting markets" and adding match/player data gives no significant improvement over odds-implied forecasts; returns "subject to high volatility and mainly negative over the longer term" across models, money-management schemes, and favourite/outsider backing; ensembles most promising. **[vs-market: models compared to odds-implied forecasts and returns computed vs bookmaker odds; bookmaker not named in abstract]** — [JSA-200463](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/JSA-200463) (abstract only; the systematic review lists Wilkens accuracy as 69–70.5%: [arXiv 2410.21484](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21484v1))

**Positive-ROI claims (all pre-match, none explicitly closing-line, none modelling exchange commission)**
- Sipko & Knottenbelt (2015, Imperial College MEng thesis): 22 features incl. fatigue/injury proxies; logistic regression and ANN; test set = 6,315 ATP matches 2013–2014 **with Pinnacle odds**, after discarding the 50% most "uncertain" matches (betting only on the least-uncertain half). ROI by strategy (Fig 7.1): Strategy 1 (bet predicted winner, flat) LR 1.46%, LR-interaction 0.73%, ANN 0.70%, Common-Opponent 0.20%; Strategy 2 (bet when p_model > p_implied) 3.62 / 1.69 / 2.07 / 1.89%; Strategy 3 (Kelly on predicted winner) 4.17 / 4.08 / **4.35** / 2.40%. Table 7.1 (Strategy 3): ANN placed 3,183 bets, invested 695.60, returned 725.85, net +30.26 units; Common-Opponent 2,515 bets, +11.75. Author notes "logistic loss and ROI often give contradictory results," ROI was "a very unstable evaluation metric," and the ROI-optimised feature selection that reached 11.3% on validation did not generalise. Odds timing (opening vs closing) not specified; no commission (Pinnacle is a bookmaker, so the margin is embedded in the odds). **[vs-market, ROI vs Pinnacle; single window; no significance test]** — [Sipko thesis PDF](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/teaching/distinguished-projects/2015/m.sipko.pdf)
- Knottenbelt, Spanias & Madurska (2012) hierarchical Markov "Common-Opponent" model: 3.8% ROI on 2,173 ATP matches from 2011 (bookmaker not named in the review). **[vs-market, ROI; older foundational]** — cited in [Sipko thesis](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/teaching/distinguished-projects/2015/m.sipko.pdf) and [arXiv 2410.21484](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21484v1)
- Lisi (2017, EJASA, "Tennis betting: can statistics beat bookmakers?"): logistic regression on ATP points/rankings, ages, home factor **plus information derived from bookmaker odds**, estimated on 2012, tested on all 501 matches of the 2013 Grand Slams: cumulative return 15.9%. Fee/margin treatment and odds timing not stated in abstract. **[vs-market, ROI; small sample; model uses the odds as an input]** — [EJASA 10(3)](https://ese-journals.unisalento.it/index.php/ejasa/article/view/16516)
- Cornman et al. (2017): random forest, 46,114 matches (Tennis-Data.co.uk odds), 69.7% CV accuracy, "3.3% profit per match." **[vs-market ROI claim; unpublished course project per review; bookmaker not stated]** — [arXiv 2410.21484](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21484v1)
- Angelini, Candila & De Angelis (2022, EJOR 297(1):120–132, "Weighted Elo rating for tennis match predictions"): WElo weights Elo updates by last-match scoreline; >60,000 men's and women's matches; "outperforms all competing methods" and "provides meaningfully profitable opportunities according to a simple betting strategy." The companion R package `welo` bets WElo/Elo probabilities against **Bet365** odds with a threshold that excludes heavy longshots. No ROI figure, sample window, or margin treatment in the abstract. **[claim-only vs Bet365]** — [cris.unibo.it](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/821483); [welo package](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/welo/refman/welo.html)
- Candila & Palazzo (2020, Risks 8(3):68, "Neural Networks and Betting Strategies for Tennis"): ANN with regression-based, point-based and paired-comparison inputs beats 4 of 5 competing models out of sample; four betting strategies; returns "more broad and robust than the best competing model." No ROI number, bookmaker, or margin stated in abstract. **[claim-only]** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jrisks/v8y2020i3p68-d377813.html)
- Gao & Kowalczyk (2021): random forest, ATP 2000–2016, 83.18% accuracy "much greater than predictions using betting odds alone" — inconsistent with every other benchmark (bookmakers ~70%); treat as suspect (likely leakage or non-comparable sample). **[outcome-only]** — [arXiv 2410.21484](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21484v1)

**Market-efficiency tests on tennis exchanges/bookmakers (rules-based, not model-based)**
- Lyócsa & Výrost (2018, Applied Economics 50(20):2251–2272, "To bet or not to bet: a reality check for tennis betting market efficiency"): 40 betting rules tested for return differentials and profitability; individual tests suggested positive returns, but once **data-snooping bias** is controlled, the evidence "diminishes"; "very little evidence of return differentials between betting rules"; conclusion: the tennis market is "much more efficient than suggested by previous studies." Keywords include Bradley–Terry. **[vs-market; bookmaker not confirmed]** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/applec/v50y2018i20p2251-2272.html)
- Lobão & Rolla (2015, RAE 55(4)): Betfair tennis exchange data; prices "generally forecast outcomes well" but bettors "respond poorly to new information" and over-weight players dominating a match; "several lucrative betting strategies were found." Commission treatment not visible in abstract. **[vs-market, exchange]** — [RAE](https://periodicos.fgv.br/rae/article/view/52958)
- Spann & Skiera (2008 foundational, Journal of Forecasting): prediction markets and betting odds equally accurate, both far better than tipsters, but none yield systematic gains because of the 25% fee of the German state bookmaker. **[vs-market after fees; football]** — [IAOR summary](https://iaorifors.com/paper/64997)

### Inferences
- The tennis literature has a stable pattern: the best public models (Elo family, Bradley–Terry, Markov/point-level, GNN) land 0.5–3 accuracy points and ~0.005–0.02 Brier *behind* Pinnacle pre-match prices on large samples, and essentially tie only on small Grand-Slam-only samples where favourites dominate.
- Every positive ROI reported is (a) a single 1–2-year window, (b) against bookmaker (not exchange) odds whose timing is unspecified, (c) without a multiple-testing correction. Sipko's own diagnostics (ROI instability; validation ROI 11.3% not reproducing) and Lyócsa & Výrost's data-snooping correction are the strongest internal evidence that these edges are not robust.
- Wilkens (2021) is the closest thing to a modern, broad replication, and it finds mainly negative long-run returns with odds as the dominant feature.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Kovalchik (2016) full text (publisher 405) — exact log-loss values per model are not in these notes.
- No tennis paper found that reports **closing-line value** (model price vs Pinnacle close) as the metric, or that nets out Betfair commission (2–5%). The Angelini et al. (2022) and Candila & Palazzo (2020) ROI numbers are behind paywalls.
- Point-level Markov models with in-play data vs in-play Betfair prices: see Q3 (Newcastle service-break study, Easton & Uylangco) — I could not fetch either full text.

## Key question 2 — Which models beat MLB market prices?

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed or working-paper evidence of a public-data MLB model beating closing moneylines after vig. The best-powered study (Choe & Ramdas, 25,165 games 2010–2019) shows Vegas closing odds beat FiveThirtyEight's Elo+pitcher model and every other baseline on Brier and log score, with anytime-valid confidence sequences. The ML-for-MLB literature is almost entirely outcome-only accuracy (55–66% for honest models), with one tiny Bet365-benchmarked study.

### Cited Findings
- Choe & Ramdas (arXiv 2110.00115, "Comparing Sequential Forecasters," published in Operations Research): all regular-season and postseason MLB games 2010–2019, T = 25,165. Forecasters: 538 (FiveThirtyEight game-by-game Elo with starting-pitcher adjustment), vegas (pre-game **closing** odds from Vegas-Odds.com converted and scaled to probabilities), constant 0.5, seasonally adjusted Laplace win%, K29. Result: vegas "marginally outperformed" 538 — 95% empirical-Bernstein confidence sequence for the average Brier differential (538 minus vegas) = (−0.00265, −0.00062); e-value for the null "538 ≥ vegas" = 2,979. Against vegas, Brier CS for laplace (−0.00980, −0.00596), k29 (−0.01392, −0.00905), constant (−0.01115, −0.00713); Winkler (log-score) upper bounds all negative (538: −0.01012). "None of the other forecasters, including 538, have outperformed vegas from 2010 to 2019." **[vs-market, Brier/log vs closing odds; no vig adjustment beyond normalisation]** — [arXiv 2110.00115](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00115)
- Systematic review (arXiv 2410.21484, §4.6 Table 8) baseball game-outcome studies: Chang (2021) Markov/runner-advancement model, **70 MLB games Sept 2018**, 75% accuracy, compared against **Bet365** odds with a "new implied probability" normalisation; Elfrink (2018) RF/XGBoost on Retrosheet 1930–2016, 55.52%, author states results "not yet robust enough to surpass betting companies"; Cui (2020) elastic-net LR, train 2001–2015/test 2016–2019, 61.77%, AUC 0.67; Li et al. (2022) SVM 2015–2019, 65.75%; Hughes (2022) LR on Lahman/Baseball Savant (Statcast), 56.6%, Brier 0.244; Huang & Li (2021) 93–94% (not credible for game outcomes — likely leakage); Yaseen et al. (2022) 60–77%. **No baseball study in the review reports ROI; only Chang uses a bookmaker benchmark.** **[outcome-only except Chang]** — [arXiv 2410.21484](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21484v1)
- Bouchard (2019, Harvard bachelor's thesis, "Information and Market Efficiency: Evidence from the MLB Betting Market"): >88,000 MLB games 1977–2018 moneylines; forecast accuracy of the market improved over time with the largest step in the late 1990s–early 2000s; incorporating current-season performance improves accuracy; **larger relative open-to-close line movements are on average less accurate** (interpreted as over-reaction). No strategy returns. **[vs-market, descriptive]** — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/434dc6e1-ce30-401a-9a71-4fd161085ec9)
- Erasmus thesis (2013) on MLB: probit with public data found no abnormal returns "except when the money lines of bookmakers were included in the model," yielding a small profit; no underdog/home underpricing found. **[vs-market ROI; student thesis]** — [thesis.eur.nl 13879](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/13879/13879-Lock.pdf)
- Allen & Savala (arXiv 2511.02815, "Assessing win strength in MLB win prediction models"): ML models benchmarked against FiveThirtyEight Elo, not Vegas; logistic regression best. **[outcome-only]** — [arXiv 2511.02815](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02815v1)
- Vendor claim (not independent, no methodology): "good sport ai" states it beat the MLB closing line 82% of the time while MLB game lines were "about break-even over the last 90 days." **[claim-only; marketing]** — [mcpservers.org listing](https://mcpservers.org/servers/good-sport-ai)

### Inferences
- The MLB market's edge over 538 is small (~0.002 Brier) but statistically unambiguous over 25k games; a public model would need to be better than 538 by more than that *and* clear a ~2–4.5% moneyline vig. No paper shows this.
- Bouchard's open-to-close finding (big moves are less accurate) is the one MLB-specific hint of an exploitable pattern (fading large line moves), but it is a thesis result with no return calculation.
- Starting-pitcher, umpire, park and weather overlays: I found **no** academic test of their incremental value against the closing line; all umpire/weather material is betting-media content (Covers, Action Network) without significance testing.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed MLB study reporting CLV or after-vig ROI for any model family (Statcast, pitcher, umpire, weather, line-movement).
- Lopez, Matthews & Baumer (2018, AOAS) use betting odds to estimate MLB randomness but I did not retrieve it; it is not a model-vs-market profit study.

## Key question 3 — The "wisdom of bookmakers" result: is the sharp closing line unbeatable, and when does it fail?

### Takeaway
Evidence across sports supports closing-line efficiency against public-data models, with two documented failure modes: (1) **cross-bookmaker dispersion** — outlier (soft) prices relative to the consensus are exploitable (Kaunitz et al., football, +3.5% simulated, +8.5% live, then accounts limited), and (2) **in-play latency/under-reaction** — exchange prices lag clean news (first goal in football, service breaks in tennis), with mispricing largest when the news is unexpected. Hubáček & Šír add a third, more subtle route: profit does not require beating the market on accuracy, only being *decorrelated* from it where it errs (NBA, +1–1.7% per unit staked vs Pinnacle closing, with lower accuracy than the bookmaker).

### Cited Findings
- Hubáček & Šír ("Beating the market with a bad predictive model," arXiv 2010.12508; IJF 39(2):691–719, 2023): NBA 2000–2014 box-score data; **Pinnacle closing odds 2010–2014** (margin ≈2.5%), multi-bookmaker odds 2000–2010 (margin ≈4.5%); evaluation seasons 2006–2014, walk-forward. Bookmaker accuracy (favourite wins) 69 ± 2.5%, "generally superior to the neural models"; model–bookmaker Pearson correlation 0.87 without odds feature, 0.95 with. Table 3 (mean profit ± SE over 10 runs, per-season units): with decorrelation weight γ=0.4 the Sharpe-style portfolio strategy earned **+1.74 ± 0.14** (no odds feature; accuracy 67.15%) and +1.49 ± 0.10 (with odds; 67.48%), vs +0.38 ± 0.10 / −0.12 ± 0.24 at γ=0 and −1.92 / −1.22 at γ=1 (accuracy 61%); uniform staking was negative at every γ except near zero. Authors explicitly note closing odds are "more difficult to beat" than opening and that bookmaker calibration curves follow the true distribution "very tightly" once small-sample artefacts at long odds are smoothed. **[vs-market, profit after bookmaker margin vs Pinnacle close; NBA not tennis/MLB]** — [arXiv 2010.12508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.12508); [IJF listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v35y2019i2p783-796.html) (companion 2019 IJF paper "Exploiting sports-betting market using machine learning," NBA 2007–2014, CNN + decorrelation + portfolio theory, positive cumulative profit)
- Kaunitz, Zhong & Kriener (arXiv 1710.02824, "Beating the bookies with their own numbers"): ~480k football matches, 32 bookmakers, Jan 2005–Jun 2015; consensus closing probability is well calibrated in 100-game bins; betting the bookmaker whose odds are an outlier above the consensus returned **+3.5%** in simulation vs −3.32% for random bets; live betting (~$50 stakes, ~30 bets/week, up to 5 months) was profitable (press reports +8.5%) with "probability of a return at least this large from random betting less than 1 in a billion"; bookmakers then **limited the accounts**. **[vs-market, ROI after margin; football; execution risk documented]** — [arXiv 1710.02824](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.02824); [MIT Tech Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2017/10/19/67760/the-secret-betting-strategy-that-beats-online-bookmakers/)
- Angelini, De Angelis & Singleton (2022, IJF 38(1):282–299, "Informational efficiency and behaviour within in-play prediction markets"): betting-exchange football data around the first goal; find pre-match and in-play mispricing explained by **reverse favourite-longshot (favourite) bias**, with mispricing growing when news is unexpected (e.g., market underestimates a longshot's chance after it scores late). **[vs-market, in-play exchange; football]** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v38y2022i1p282-299.html); [working paper PDF](https://reading-9.eprints-hosting.org/98329/1/information_efficiency_angelini_de_angelis_singleton.pdf)
- Newcastle (Australia) study of in-play tennis odds: odds "fail to capture the tendency of players to lose more points than expected after conceding a break of service instantaneously," while the significance of breaks/holds is anticipated up to four points before game end; no comparable lag after a hold. **[vs-market, in-play; could not fetch full text]** — [Newcastle repository uon:9686](https://ogma.newcastle.edu.au/vital/access/manager/Repository/uon:9686)
- Easton & Uylangco (QUT), "Forecasting outcomes in tennis matches using within-match betting markets": point-level model vs within-match exchange prices. **[could not fetch; listed for completeness]** — [QUT eprints 217966](https://eprints.qut.edu.au/217966)
- Porto thesis (2012–13 data, 1,910 observations from 203 matches): betting against the in-play favourite "when he is winning" produced positive returns (behavioural explanation). **[vs-market; student thesis; commission treatment unknown]** — [repositorio-aberto.up.pt](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/69360/2/25061.pdf)
- Franck, Verbeek & Nüesch (2010, IJF, foundational): 5,478 Big-Five football matches; the betting exchange (Betfair) was more accurate than bookmakers. **[vs-market comparison between venues]** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v26yi3p448-459.html)
- Brown & Reade (2019, EJOR, "The wisdom of amateur crowds"): aggregated Oddsportal tipster picks contain information not in prices; majority-pick strategy average return **+1.317% over 68,339 events**. **[vs-market ROI]** — [UEA eprints](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/67529)
- Industry latency figure: operators run in-play on streams 5–8 seconds behind the event and compensate by suspending markets earlier (Stats Perform via SBC News, trade press). **[claim-only, non-academic]** — [SBC News](https://sbcnews.co.uk/latestnews/2026/05/20/cost-stream-delay-live-betting/)
- Antwerp dissertation reviewing 40 years of mechanical trading strategies in sports prediction markets: many individual studies claim profits, but "the broader body of evidence fits an efficient market in which apparent profits are chance results." **[meta-level]** — [University of Antwerp repository](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/link/irua/188493)

### Inferences
- The closing line of a sharp book is empirically the best single public forecast in every sport tested (tennis, MLB, NBA, football). The exploitable residue is not in beating it on accuracy but in (a) price dispersion across venues, (b) temporal lags (early lines, in-play reaction time), and (c) decorrelated errors — all of which are capacity-limited and, for (a), met with account limiting.
- "Bad model, good decorrelation" (Hubáček & Šír) is the most directly actionable academic result for a model-vs-exchange bot: it reframes the objective as maximising covariance with market error, not accuracy; the measured edge (~1.5–1.7 units/season over 2.5% margin) is small and strategy-dependent.

### Gaps
- No direct academic test of Pinnacle closing vs opening calibration in tennis or MLB (only Bouchard's MLB line-movement result and Hubáček's assertion).
- In-play tennis latency arbitrage is documented only in theses, one unretrieved Newcastle paper and trade press; no peer-reviewed after-commission return.

## Key question 4 — What do reviews/meta-analyses (Hubáček & co., Wunderlich & Memmert, Štrumbelj) conclude about which features carry information the market lacks?

### Takeaway
The reviews converge on: (1) bookmaker odds are the single most informative feature and are best de-vigged with Shin's method (Štrumbelj 2014); (2) accuracy and profitability are distinct objectives — profit comes from calibration/decorrelation relative to the market, and positive returns can arise by chance from inaccurate models (Wunderlich & Memmert 2020); (3) the systematic review finds that most published "profitable" tennis/MLB models never benchmark against bookmaker probabilities at all.

### Cited Findings
- Štrumbelj (2014, IJF, "On determining probability forecasts from betting odds"): implied probabilities from Shin's model predict outcomes better than basic normalisation; one of the few applications of Shin to soccer; regression-based de-vigging also considered. Later work: Shin gives unbiased probabilities for the EPL but both normalisation and Shin show favourite bias in La Liga (Annals of OR 2022). **[vs-market calibration]** — [Springer s10479-022-04722-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10479-022-04722-3); [implied R package](https://www.r-bloggers.com/the-probabilities-implied-by-bookmaker-odds-introducing-the-implied-package/); [shin Python package](https://pypi.org/project/shin)
- Wunderlich & Memmert (2020, IJF 36(2):713–722, "Are betting returns a useful measure of accuracy in (sports) forecasting?"): theory + simulation + real data from three sports show "positive betting returns can arise either consistently or by chance even when a model is not more accurate than alternatives"; "betting returns should not be treated as a valid measure of model accuracy." Wunderlich's dissertation adds: pre-game odds contain more information about team strength than actual results; first-half goals are "clearly outperformed by betting odds" for forecasting second-half goals. **[methodological]** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v36y2020i2p713-722.html); [Dissertation PDF](https://fis-db.dshs-koeln.de/ws/portalfiles/portal/6465170/Dissertation_Wunderlich.pdf)
- Wunderlich & Memmert (2018, PLOS ONE, "The Betting Odds Rating System"): rating teams from odds rather than results improves forecasts — i.e., odds are a better *input* than outcomes. **[vs-market as feature]** — cited in [Wunderlich dissertation](https://fis-db.dshs-koeln.de/ws/portalfiles/portal/6465170/Dissertation_Wunderlich.pdf)
- Hubáček, Šourek & Železný (2019, IJF 35(2):783–796): previous work "maximized the model's predictive accuracy as the single criterion"; reducing correlation with the bookmaker's odds "allows for better profit generation"; CNN over player-level stats + modern-portfolio-theory stake distribution; positive cumulative profit NBA 2007–2014. Hubáček's 2024 PhD thesis ("Sport prediction market modeling with portfolio optimization") extends this to trading on prediction markets. **[vs-market, NBA]** — [CTU paper page](https://ida.fel.cvut.cz/papers/hubacek2019exploiting.html); [PhD thesis PDF](https://dspace.cvut.cz/bitstream/handle/10467/114141/F3-D-2024-Hubacek-Ondrej-hubacek_thesis.pdf)
- Systematic review of ML in sports betting (arXiv 2410.21484, 2024): of ten tennis studies tabulated, only Wilkens (2021) benchmarks against bookmaker-implied probabilities and finds no significant advantage; of eight MLB studies, only Chang (2021, 70 games) uses a bookmaker benchmark; none report ROI. The review cites Walsh & Joshi (2024, Machine Learning with Applications, "should model selection be based on accuracy or calibration?") where a calibration-selected model produced **69.86% higher average returns** than the accuracy-selected one (NBA). **[meta]** — [arXiv 2410.21484](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21484v1); [Walsh & Joshi](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/machine-learning-for-sports-betting-should-model-selection-be-bas/)
- Wilkens (2021): "irrespective of the used model, most of the relevant information is embedded in the betting markets" (tennis). **[vs-market feature-importance]** — [JSA-200463](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/JSA-200463)
- Sipko (2015): minimal-log-loss model is "not always the most profitable"; removing the 20% most uncertain matches improved both ROI and log-loss, but ROI dropped sharply past the 80% cut while log-loss kept improving. **[vs-market]** — [Sipko thesis](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/teaching/distinguished-projects/2015/m.sipko.pdf)

### Inferences
- Across reviews, the features that *do* seem to carry information the market lacks are not player statistics but market-structural ones: (i) dispersion across bookmakers, (ii) crowd/tipster aggregates, (iii) scoreline-weighted ratings (WElo) — and even these are small and claim-level for tennis.
- The methodological consensus (W&M 2020; Hubáček & Šír 2023; Walsh & Joshi 2024; Sipko's own experience) is that models for trading should be selected on calibration/decorrelation against the market price, not accuracy — and that any ROI from a single window needs a data-snooping correction before being believed.

### Gaps
- Štrumbelj (2014) full text not retrieved; exact improvement of Shin over normalisation not quantified here.
- No meta-analysis pools tennis or MLB ROI results with fee accounting; the arXiv review tabulates but does not adjust.

## Key question 5 — Evidence specific to prediction-market venues (Polymarket, Kalshi, PredictIt): efficiency, calibration, exploitable biases after fees

### Takeaway
Working papers from 2025–2026 find Polymarket/Kalshi sports prices well calibrated mid-life, with the favourite-longshot bias present platform-wide on Polymarket (longshots <10c lose 19.3c/$) but **absent in Sports**; Kalshi sports show a step-like calibration distortion in the last ~10 minutes before expiry and systematic parlay over-pricing that grows with leg count. None of these papers tests profitability after fees; fee structures (Polymarket taker fee ≈ rate × p(1−p), up to 0.05 on sports since 2026) fall hardest on exactly the longshot side a bias strategy would need.

### Cited Findings
- Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe (arXiv 2609.12878, Sept 2026, "The Favorite-Longshot Bias in Prediction Markets: Evidence from Polymarket"): 588M trades, 2.48M accounts. Purchases below 10c lose **19.3c per dollar**; purchases ≥90c earn **+0.83c per dollar**. Grouping matters: longshots lose 6.3c/$ equal-weighted by contract but *gain* 4.1c/$ when related contracts are grouped by parent event. The two-sided pattern is "robust in Crypto and Politics but surprisingly absent in Sports." Top-decile longshot buyers account for 26.6% of next-month low-price purchases but earn returns similar to others; top-decile favourite buyers earn less than others. Fees and after-fee profitability not addressed in abstract. **[vs-market, realised returns by price bucket; no fee netting]** — [arXiv 2609.12878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12878)
- Moshrefi (arXiv 2607.14430, July 2026, "Prices, Probabilities, and Parlays: Systematic Bias in Sports Prediction Markets"): **23 million Kalshi moneyline trades**, major leagues. Calibration parameters sit at perfect-calibration reference values mid-life but "depart sharply as expiry approaches"; in the final ten minutes the calibration curve becomes step-like (Prelec curvature well above 1), attributed to insurance-style buying by traders holding losing positions. Cross-game parlays are priced above the product of contemporaneous leg prices, with over-pricing growing in leg count, persisting even when legs are from the well-calibrated mid-life regime — read as a markup at the parlay-pricing stage. Fees and tradability of the bias not tested. **[vs-market calibration; no fee netting]** — [arXiv 2607.14430](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14430)
- Le (arXiv 2602.19520, 2026, "Decomposing Crowd Wisdom"): 353M trades across 429k binary contracts on Kalshi and Polymarket (5–95c, thin markets excluded); sports prices "track nearer the diagonal" (closer to calibrated) than politics, which shows persistent under-confidence on both venues; decomposition explains 87.3% in-sample / 71.5% out-of-sample variance on Kalshi. **[vs-market calibration]** — [arXiv 2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.19520)
- Kalshi's own calibration report: prices "well calibrated and accurate overall," improving as resolution approaches; calibration depends on time-to-resolution, volume and trader count; "re-scaling expiration times unexpectedly improves calibration in Sports." (Company-authored; could not fetch — rate-limited.) **[claim-only, self-published]** — [kalshi-research-calibration.pdf](https://kalshi.com/research/kalshi-research-calibration.pdf)
- Clinton & Huang (Vanderbilt, reported Dec 2025): ~2,500 political markets (~$2.5B volume) on Polymarket, Kalshi, PredictIt during the final five weeks of the 2024 US election; "accuracy" (odds-to-outcome match) PredictIt 93%, Kalshi 78%, Polymarket 67%; 58% of Polymarket national presidential markets showed negative daily serial correlation (over-reaction/noise trading); inefficiency rose in the final two weeks; Polymarket daily moves barely correlated with Kalshi/PredictIt. Kalshi disputes the methodology. **Politics only — no sports.** **[vs-outcome, log-loss/Brier; non-sports]** — [DL News](https://www.dlnews.com/articles/markets/polymarket-kalshi-prediction-markets-not-so-reliable-says-study)
- SimpleFunctions tracker: across 928 resolved Kalshi+Polymarket markets, price 24h before settlement had Brier 0.213; sample dominated by crypto and sports bracket markets. **[non-academic, small]** — [simplefunctions.dev/calibration](https://simplefunctions.dev/calibration)
- Polymarket fee facts (secondary sources, conflicting): taker-only fees, zero maker fees, part rebated to makers; sports fee rate moved from 0.03 to 0.05 in 2026, max taker fee from $0.75 to $1.25 per 100 shares; quoted "max effective rate" for sports 0.44% vs 0.75% in different sources. Sports volume showed no decline after rollout. **[non-academic]** — [Pine Analytics](https://pineanalytics.substack.com/p/polymarket-fee-rollout); [Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/polymarket-at-375m-year-revenue/); [startpolymarket fees](https://startpolymarket.com/learn/polymarket-fees/)
- On-chain analysis (Dec 2025): ~70% of Polymarket traders lose money; <0.04% of accounts capture >70% of profits; not broken out by sports. **[non-academic]** — [coin360](https://coin360.com/news/polymarket-traders-losses-profit-concentration-2026-predictions)
- A dev.to post claims Polymarket sports favourites are over-priced by 8–14% on 487 markets — no method, contradicts Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe; discount it. — [dev.to](https://dev.to/edgelab/the-wisdom-of-crowds-is-wrong-about-sports-23-of-the-time-heres-the-pattern-jun-30-55i8)
- Older foundational: Spann & Skiera (2008) — prediction markets and bookmaker odds equally accurate; neither profitable after a 25% fee. — [IAOR](https://iaorifors.com/paper/64997)

### Inferences
- For a tennis/MLB moneyline bot on Polymarket/Kalshi, the academically documented biases are (a) near-expiry (last ~10 min) calibration distortion on Kalshi sports and (b) parlay markup — both are *in-play / derivative* phenomena, not pre-match moneyline mispricing. Pre-match sports moneylines appear close to calibrated and free of the longshot bias that exists elsewhere on Polymarket.
- Fee geometry (≈ rate × p(1−p) per share) means a 10c longshot pays roughly 9× the cost-proportional fee of a 90c favourite; any strategy relying on longshot or near-expiry mispricing must clear that.
- No paper tests whether a sportsbook-closing-line-derived fair value beats Polymarket/Kalshi sports prices after fees — this is the single most relevant gap for the repo's use case.

### Gaps

- No study measures Polymarket/Kalshi sports prices against Pinnacle closing lines (cross-venue CLV), nor liquidity/slippage at exchange depth.
